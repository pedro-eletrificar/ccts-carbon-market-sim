How to play the CCTS Carbon Market Simulator

You run one company inside India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) — the intensity-based baseline-and-credit carbon market notified under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022. This guide covers what the scheme means for your company, how each phase of a compliance year works, who the other eight operators are, and what's real versus illustrative in this simulation.

1. What CCTS actually is

The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme is India's national carbon market, built on the same Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) infrastructure that ran the older PAT (Perform, Achieve, Trade) energy-efficiency scheme. It has two tiers: a compliance mechanism for roughly 740 obligated entities across nine energy-intensive sectors, and a separate voluntary offset mechanism for everyone else. This simulator models the compliance mechanism — the one with legally binding targets.

Unlike a cap-and-trade scheme, CCTS does not hand out a fixed tonnage cap. It assigns each entity a target emission intensity — tonnes of CO2-equivalent per tonne of product — measured against a FY2023-24 baseline. Beat your target and the surplus becomes tradeable Carbon Credit Certificates (CCCs). Miss it, and you must surrender CCCs to cover the gap, or pay a penalty.

Why intensity, not a hard cap

An intensity target lets output grow without automatically breaching the scheme — a cement plant that produces more cement isn't punished for it, only for producing each tonne more carbon-intensively than its target allows. That's also why your target moves with your own output in this simulator: it's tonnes of CO2e per tonne of product, not a fixed annual ceiling.

2. Quick start

  1. Register a company. Pick one of the nine CCTS sectors, then pick a starting posture: Efficient Incumbent, Mid-Pack Operator, or Legacy Laggard.
  2. Play a full three-year compliance cycle — FY 2025-26, FY 2026-27, and FY 2027-28, each stricter than the last — with an abatement decision and a trading window every year.
  3. See where you land on the nine-operator leaderboard, ranked by cumulative profit, alongside eight AI-driven counterparts.

First time here? The landing page offers a short guided tour — five dismissible tip cards that walk through registration, briefing, abatement, trading, and settlement on your first playthrough. You can replay it any time from the footnote link.

3. Your company

Every company has a baseline intensity (tCO2e per unit of output, fixed at FY2023-24 levels) and an annual output volume. Your starting posture shifts both your baseline and how expensive further cuts are:

PostureBaseline intensityRemaining abatement
Efficient IncumbentBelow sector averageExpensive — the cheap fixes are already done
Mid-Pack OperatorSector averageBalanced cost curve
Legacy LaggardAbove sector averageCheap headroom still available

There's a real strategic tension here: an efficient incumbent has less distance to its target in absolute terms, but each further tonne of abatement costs more — sometimes more than it's worth relative to just buying CCCs. A laggard has further to travel but can often get there cheaply. Neither posture is strictly better; it depends on how the market prices carbon.

4. The four phases of a compliance year

Each of the three compliance years (FY 2025-26, FY 2026-27, and FY 2027-28) runs through the same four phases:

01

Target Briefing

BEE notifies your sector's intensity trajectory. You see your baseline, the required cumulative cut, and your resulting target intensity for the year.

02

Abatement Plan

Decide how many measures on your cost curve to commit to, and how much of your capacity to run given the year's demand outlook (see section 5). Abatement sets how much capital you spend and, with a delay, your actual intensity; production sets your output and revenue.

03

Exchange

Post a buy or sell order for CCCs. A uniform clearing price is set across all nine operators' orders, bounded by the price collar.

04

Settlement

Trades execute, shortfalls are covered from your bank or penalized, surplus is sold or banked, and your profit for the period is booked.

Each year's targets are more demanding than the last — this mirrors CCTS's real phased trajectory, which tightens from roughly a 2-3% average cut in FY25-26 to 3.3-7.5%+ in FY26-27. FY27-28 continues that trajectory (see section 13 for exactly how).

5. Demand scenarios and production planning

At the start of every compliance year, before you plan abatement, a macro growth scenario is drawn and shown to every operator on the Target Briefing screen — a coherent story (an infrastructure boom, an export slowdown, a broad recession) that moves several related sectors' demand together, rather than each sector getting independent random noise. The same scenario applies to everyone in the room that year, so in multiplayer it's common ground every player is reasoning against.

Your output capacity is fixed — set once when your company was generated and never changes. What you decide each year is your production level: what share of that fixed capacity you actually run, from 70% to 130%, given the year's demand outlook for your sector.

Intensity targets don't care how much you produce — but they still bite harder

CCTS targets are per-unit (tCO2e per tonne of output), not an absolute cap. Producing more doesn't change whether you're compliant on an intensity basis — but it does scale up the tonnage of whatever surplus or deficit you already have. A small per-unit shortfall that's easy to shrug off at 100% production becomes a real, expensive deficit at 130%. Production is a multiplier on the compliance position your abatement plan already put you in, for better or worse.

Production also sets your revenue for the year. Every sector has a demand ceiling for the year — your fixed capacity times the scenario's demand multiplier for that sector. Output up to that ceiling sells at full margin; anything produced past it still gets made (and still emits) but sells at a steep discount, representing dumping into a market that doesn't want it. The Abatement Plan screen's production slider previews this live: actual output, projected revenue, and a warning the moment you cross the ceiling.

The scenario card shows every sector's demand multiplier for the year, not just yours — useful context for reading what your trading counterparts are likely to do (a sector staring at -10% demand has less reason to run its capacity hard, whatever its compliance position).

6. CBAM: the EU border adjustment (optional module)

An optional layer you can switch on at setup (or, in Multiplayer, that the host switches on for the room): the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, sitting alongside CCTS rather than replacing any of it. If it's on and your sector is Aluminium, Iron & Steel, Cement, or Fertilizer — the four of this simulator's nine sectors that the real CBAM currently covers — you'll see a new card on the Abatement Plan screen each year.

A second, independent carbon cost on the same tonnes

CBAM is not a CCTS mechanic wearing a different name — it's the EU charging its own certificate cost on the carbon embedded in whatever share of your output you sell there, calculated at an EU reference price that's deliberately modeled well above the CCTS collar. It exists alongside your CCTS obligation, not instead of it.

Each year the card asks two things:

  • What share of this year's output goes to the EU (0-130% production, 0-100% of that sent to EU customers). The rest is unaffected by CBAM.
  • Whether to submit verified emissions data. Verifying uses your real, already-computed intensity and costs a flat MRV/verification fee. Skipping it means the EU falls back to a deliberately punitive default value — always worse than your true figure — with no fee. Which is cheaper depends on how much you're exporting.

The certificate cost itself follows the real formula: embedded emissions (intensity × EU-bound output) × this year's CBAM phase-in obligation fraction (starting low — 2.5% in Year 1 — and rising toward the real regulation's 2034 full-implementation point) × the EU reference price, minus whatever's deductible.

Does your CCTS compliance even count?

That deduction is the module's sharpest edge. Real-world CBAM (Article 9) lets an importer deduct a "carbon price effectively paid" in the country of origin — but whether India's CCTS qualifies is a genuinely unresolved question, not a settled fact: CCTS only charges non-compliant entities, so a fully compliant company has no per-tonne payment to point to. At setup, choose which assumption to play under. Article 9 recognized deducts this year's CCTS clearing price from the EU reference price before charging you. Article 9 not recognized — closer to where the real policy debate currently sits — charges the full amount regardless of what CCTS already cost you. Playing both ways back to back is the fastest way to feel the difference.

Settlement adds a matching set of rows — EU-bound output, embedded emissions (flagged verified or default-value), certificates surrendered, and total CBAM cost — right alongside your existing CCTS settlement. Turn the module off at setup and every one of these rows, and the decision itself, simply disappears; nothing else in the game changes.

7. Reading your abatement cost curve

Every company has four abatement measures, ordered cheapest-to-most-expensive — a simplified marginal abatement cost curve (MACC). Each measure shows two numbers: how much intensity it cuts (tCO2e per unit of output) and what it costs per tonne abated (₹/tCO2e).

Grounded in real technical-committee MACC data

For eight of the nine sectors — every one except Fertilizer, which has no such study in this build's source material — these four tiers are derived directly from NSCICM/BEE technical-committee MACC workbooks: every real named technology in that sector's workbook (10 to 25 per sector), sorted cheapest to most expensive, cut into four equal-potential blocks. What changes tier to tier is the real cost curve shape; the sector's baseline intensity is that same workbook's disclosed FY23-24 figure. Fertilizer, with no workbook available, keeps the earlier illustrative cost curve.

The real curves open with genuinely cost-negative measures in several sectors — an efficiency retrofit or waste-heat-recovery project that pays for itself while also cutting emissions, a real feature of real MACCs, not a display bug. When a measure's ₹/tCO2e is negative, the Abatement Plan screen shows it as savings rather than a negative currency figure.

The abatement slider commits measures in order — you can't skip to an expensive measure without first committing the cheaper ones ahead of it, which mirrors how real efficiency investment tends to work (the cheap retrofit happens before the expensive process redesign).

Investments are durable — but not instant

Once a measure is committed, the capital is spent immediately — but the emissions cut doesn't land right away for anything beyond the cheapest tier. Each measure has a lead time: the Efficiency Retrofit takes effect the same year, the Fuel/Process Switch and Advanced Capture take a year to come online, and the Deep Process Redesign takes two. A measure keeps reducing your intensity for free in every year after it lands, but until then you're paying for it without the benefit — which is exactly the risk a real capital project carries.

The measure list shows you exactly where each one stands: Idle (not committed), Pending (committed, capital spent, still waiting on its lead time), or Active (actually cutting your emissions this year). The "Resulting Intensity" figure only ever counts what's Active — never what's merely committed.

This is why front-loading matters

With a three-year cycle and up to a two-year lead time, committing your biggest measure in Year 2 or later may mean it never lands before the cycle ends — you'll have paid the capex with nothing to show for it this cycle. Committing early is a real bet on the trajectory ahead, not a free action.

Watch the bullet bar on the Abatement Plan screen: the filled bar is your actual (Active-only) intensity, the vertical mark is your target. Green means you're at or under target (a surplus); red means you're still short (a deficit you'll need to cover on the exchange).

8. How the exchange clears a price

CCTS trades CCCs through power exchanges under Central Electricity Regulatory Commission oversight, with a price collar mechanism planned. This simulator models that as a uniform-price call auction: every one of the nine operators — you and eight AI-driven counterparts — submits one order for the trading window, all at once. Sellers state a minimum acceptable price; buyers state a maximum they'll pay.

The system finds the single clearing price where the most volume crosses — every filled seller receives that price, every filled buyer pays that price, regardless of what they originally asked for. The result is clamped to the collar: ₹500–₹6,000 per tCO2e in this simulation.

  • If you have a surplus, you choose how much to offer for sale and your floor price. Anything you don't offer — or offer but don't sell — is banked automatically.
  • If you have a deficit, your bank balance is drawn down first, automatically, before you even reach the trading screen. You then choose how much of the remainder to buy and your ceiling price.
  • If the market simply doesn't cross — nobody's bid reaches anybody's ask — no trade happens and the reference price for the next window falls back to the last real clearing price.

9. Settlement and your score

After the exchange closes, each operator settles independently:

  1. Any deficit not covered by the bank draw-down or exchange purchase is penalized at 2× the clearing price per tonne short — this is CCTS's actual notified penalty rate.
  2. Any surplus not sold is banked. Banking is unlimited and never expires; borrowing from a future year is never allowed — both match the real scheme.
  3. Your profit for the period is: production revenue (at full margin up to the year's demand ceiling, a discounted margin on anything sold past it — see section 5), minus this period's new abatement capex, minus net CCC spend (what you paid to buy, less what you earned selling), minus any penalty, minus any CBAM certificate and verification cost if that module is on (see section 6).

Cumulative profit across all three years determines your rank on the nine-operator leaderboard at the end of the game.

The final results screen also includes a debrief comparing your actual cumulative profit to what a "Cost-Minimizing Complier" running the same sector would have made instead — the same baseline strategy described in section 10, replayed against the real market prices and demand scenarios you played through. It's an idealized comparison (full liquidity, no penalty, no banking friction assumed for the counterfactual), not a full re-simulation of the auction with everyone else's behavior changed too — but it's a useful gut check on whether your strategy actually beat the safe play, and by how much.

10. The other eight operators

The eight AI-driven companies are drawn from four recurring strategies, so the market has real texture rather than one uniform opponent:

Cost-Minimizing Complier
Activates just enough measures to hit its target with a small buffer — no more, no less. The market's baseline "sensible" behavior.
Banker / Speculator
Activates any measure cheaper than its price expectation, even beyond what's needed to comply, aiming to bank or sell the surplus. Sets a high floor price when selling, betting the market will pay for it.
Aggressive Early-Abater
Front-loads measures early to lock in cheap cuts before targets tighten, then sells its surplus fairly aggressively to realize the gain.
Laggard Penalty-Payer
Activates the bare minimum and sets a low ceiling price when buying — it would often rather eat the 2× penalty than pay what the market is asking. Watch for this type keeping the exchange from clearing at all.

Every AI operator also sets its own production level each year — roughly tracking its sector's demand multiplier for the scenario, plus a little noise. Bots don't chase the oversupply discount, but they also won't leave demand on the table by running well under the ceiling.

11. Strategy notes

  • Check whether a measure is worth it before you commit it — compare its ₹/tCO2e cost to what CCCs are actually trading for. A measure priced above the market is cheaper to skip and buy your way out of instead.
  • Front-loading is a bet on the whole trajectory, not just next year. Committing your Deep Process Redesign in Year 1 is close to the last moment it can still land before the cycle ends (2-year lead time, 3-year cycle) — wait too long and you're paying for a measure that never delivers this cycle.
  • Lead time turns "reactive" compliance into a trap. Waiting until you see a deficit to commit a measure means that measure's cut arrives one or two years after the year you needed it — plan against next year's target, not this year's.
  • Production amplifies whatever compliance position you're already in. Running hard in a boom year is free money only if your abatement plan already covers the target — otherwise you're scaling up a deficit right alongside the revenue.
  • Chasing every unit of demand isn't always worth it. Producing past the year's demand ceiling still emits at full intensity but sells at a 40% discount — sometimes the better call is to under-produce a soft-demand sector rather than dump into it.
  • A big ask doesn't guarantee a sale. If your floor price sits above every buyer's ceiling, nothing trades and your surplus just gets banked instead — not a loss, but not realized cash either.
  • The penalty is a real backstop, not a bluff. At 2× the clearing price, letting a deficit ride to settlement is rarely cheaper than covering it on the exchange unless the market is asking an unusually high price.

12. Glossary

CCC — Carbon Credit Certificate
The tradeable unit of the compliance mechanism. 1 CCC = 1 tonne of CO2-equivalent.
Intensity target
Your required emissions per unit of output for a given compliance year, expressed in tCO2e per tonne (or equivalent unit) of product.
Baseline
Your FY2023-24 emission intensity — the fixed reference point every year's target is cut from.
MACC — Marginal Abatement Cost Curve
The ordered list of abatement measures available to a company, cheapest to most expensive.
Lead time
The delay between committing capital to an abatement measure and it actually cutting emissions — 0 to 2 years in this simulator, depending on the measure.
Banking
Carrying unsold surplus CCCs forward to a future compliance year. Unlimited under CCTS.
Borrowing
Using a future year's allowance to cover this year's shortfall. Not permitted under CCTS.
Price collar
The floor and ceiling the clearing price is not allowed to move outside of — ₹500 to ₹6,000/tCO2e in this simulation.
BEE — Bureau of Energy Efficiency
The scheme administrator: sets methodologies, benchmarks, and MRV protocols.
CERC — Central Electricity Regulatory Commission
Oversees CCC trading on the power exchanges.
MRV
Monitoring, Reporting and Verification — the annual emissions-reporting and third-party-verification cycle every obligated entity goes through.
Growth scenario
A coherent macro demand story drawn at the start of each compliance year, moving several sectors' demand together rather than each in isolation. Shown to every operator before they plan production or abatement.
Production level
The share of your fixed output capacity you choose to actually run this year, 70%–130%. Scales your absolute emissions, allowance, and revenue — it does not change your per-unit intensity or target.
Demand ceiling
Your fixed capacity times the year's demand multiplier for your sector. Output up to this level sells at full margin; output past it sells at a discount.
CBAM — Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism
The EU's carbon tariff on imports, modeled here as an optional module. A second, independent carbon cost on the share of your output you sell into the EU, alongside — not instead of — your CCTS obligation.
Embedded emissions (CBAM)
Your intensity (verified, or the EU's punitive default value if you didn't verify) multiplied by your EU-bound output — the quantity the CBAM certificate obligation is calculated against.
Article 9 deduction
The real CBAM's mechanism for crediting a "carbon price effectively paid" in the country of origin against the certificate obligation. Whether CCTS qualifies is unresolved — modeled here as a scenario toggle, not a fixed assumption.

13. What's real vs. illustrative

Read this before citing any number from the game

This simulator is built for gameplay and intuition, not for policy analysis. Treat every number in it as illustrative unless stated otherwise here.

Grounded in the real scheme (as notified through June 2026):

  • The overall mechanism design — intensity-based baseline-and-credit, unlimited banking, no borrowing, 2× penalty, the two-tier compliance/offset structure.
  • The nine covered sectors and the three-year notification-period structure — CCTS sets targets in 3-year blocks, which is why this simulator runs a full three-year cycle (FY25-26 through FY27-28) rather than stopping partway through one.
  • Disclosed sector reduction ranges for Aluminium, Cement, Chlor-Alkali, Pulp & Paper, and Iron & Steel for FY25-26 and FY26-27 — these are the actual notified ranges. Iron & Steel has no notified target for FY25-26 at all — its Third Schedule (draft G.S.R. 517(E), 26 June 2026) was notified after that compliance year was already underway, so every one of its ~255 obligated entities gets "-" for that year in the official table; this simulator shows that honestly (target equals baseline in Year 1 for that sector, not a rounding of a small number) rather than inventing a figure.
  • Abatement cost curves for eight of the nine sectors — every one except Fertilizer — are built directly from real NSCICM/BEE technical-committee MACC workbooks (see Section 7): every named technology in that sector's own workbook, sorted cheapest to most expensive and cut into four equal-potential blocks, including each sector's real disclosed FY23-24 baseline intensity.

Illustrative, built for a playable game:

  • Reduction targets for the other four sectors (Fertilizer, Petroleum Refining, Petrochemicals, Textiles) are interpolated within the disclosed overall envelope for FY25-26/FY26-27, not official BEE figures — flagged as "(illustrative)" wherever they appear.
  • Every sector's FY27-28 target is extrapolated, not disclosed — CCTS has only notified two years of targets to date. It's a linear continuation of that sector's own FY26-to-FY27 trend, flagged "(extrapolated)" wherever it appears, for every sector including the five with real FY25-26/FY26-27 data. For Iron & Steel this compounds harder than for other sectors, since its whole notified trajectory is compressed into the FY26→FY27 jump alone.
  • Fertilizer's abatement cost curve — the one sector with no MACC workbook in this build's source material — is still the earlier illustrative curve (four tiers at ₹400/1,200/2,800/5,500 per tCO2e), not sourced from any real technology data.
  • Every company's baseline intensity within a sector, output volume, and production margin are procedurally generated for gameplay balance around that sector's real (or illustrative) baseline — not sourced from real company-level disclosures. A single sector-level MACC also can't capture how much real entity-level intensities vary by product line — the Iron & Steel schedule alone ranges from ~0.14 tCO2e/tonne for coated/finished products to 4-8+ for ferro-alloys around a roughly 2.8 t central baseline — the same simplification this simulator makes for every sector, just newly visible here because the real data is so heterogeneous.
  • Targets in this simulator are computed by applying each sector's cumulative reduction percentage to each company's own baseline. The real CCTS sets entity-specific targets through a more detailed BEE process — this is a simplification.
  • The ₹500–₹6,000/tCO2e price collar is a plausible illustrative range, not a published number — CCTS has stated a collar is planned but has not (as of this deck) published its bounds.
  • Abatement measure lead times (0/1/1/2 years) are an illustrative approximation, not sourced from real project-delivery timelines — the real MACC workbooks give cost and potential per technology, but not a construction/ramp-up timeline, so lead time stays a modeling assumption even for the eight sectors with real cost curves.
  • The CBAM module's certificate formula, phase-in schedule shape, and default-value markup follow the real regulation's structure, but its EU reference price, verification cost, and default-value multiplier are illustrative constants chosen for gameplay, not published EU figures. Whether CCTS qualifies for the Article 9 deduction is a genuinely open policy question at time of writing — the toggle lets you explore either answer, it doesn't assert one.
  • Growth scenarios, their per-sector demand multipliers, and the oversupply discount rate are illustrative, not sourced from any real macroeconomic forecast — they exist to give operators a coherent yearly story to plan production against, not to model actual demand risk for these industries.

If you have real investment lead times, or a MACC study for Fertilizer, they can be substituted in the same way the other eight sectors' cost curves were — ask for it to be added.

14. The Market Simulator mode

Where Solo vs. AI Operators has you playing one company by hand, the Market Simulator has no manual play at all — you configure the shape of the market and it runs the whole thing many times over, reporting a forecast rather than a single outcome.

What you configure

Firms per sector
How many simulated operators populate each of the nine sectors. Every trial regenerates the entire population from scratch — a fresh random draw of baselines, output volumes, and cost curves each time.
Starting-posture mix
Relative weight given to Efficient Incumbent, Mid-Pack Operator, and Legacy Laggard. These are odds, not fixed percentages — a weight of 100 against two weights of 50 means that posture is drawn roughly twice as often, not that it's exactly half the market.
Trading-behavior mix
Relative weight given to the four bot strategies described in section 10. This is what shapes market dynamics most: a market weighted heavily toward Laggard Penalty-Payers will show more uncovered deficits and a higher penalty share; one weighted toward Bankers will show more banked surplus and firmer prices.
Abatement cost uncertainty
How much each measure's cost and reduction potential is allowed to randomly vary firm-to-firm (±5% / ±10% / ±25%). Higher uncertainty widens the forecast band.
Monte Carlo trials
How many independent runs to average over. More trials narrow the forecast band around a more stable estimate, at the cost of a slightly longer run — the whole simulator runs client-side in your browser, so even the largest setting (300 trials × 50 firms/sector) finishes in a few seconds.

Reading the results

  • CCC clearing price is shown as a median with a 10th–90th percentile band across all trials — the band is the forecast's honest uncertainty, not noise to ignore. A very narrow band means the configuration you chose reliably produces one outcome; a wide band means the market's price is genuinely sensitive to how the population happens to be drawn.
  • Compliance rate is the average share of simulated firms that closed the year with zero uncovered shortfall.
  • Abatement delivered vs. required compares what the simulated market actually cut to what its targets implied — over 100% means the market over-complied in aggregate (which can still coexist with many individual firms being penalized, if abatement is concentrated among a few operators).
  • How covered shortfalls were closed breaks every deficit tonne, market-wide, into what fraction was covered by drawing the bank, buying on the exchange, or absorbing the 2× penalty.
  • Sector breakdown shows compliance and abatement ratios per sector — useful for spotting which sectors' targets are least achievable under the mix you configured (Pulp & Paper's disclosed FY27 target, a 15% cumulative cut, tends to be the hardest to hit).
This is a forecasting toy, not a market prediction

The output is only as credible as the behavioral weights and cost curves you put in, and the cost curves are procedurally generated (see section 13). Use it to build intuition for how a behavioral mix shapes price and compliance outcomes, not as a prediction of what the real CCTS market will do.

15. Live Multiplayer and the Facilitator Dashboard

Live Multiplayer plays out exactly like Solo vs. AI Operators, except real people claim the nine sector seats instead of AI archetypes filling all of them. One person creates a room and gets a 4-letter code; everyone else joins with that code, picks an unclaimed sector, and sets a starting posture. Any seat nobody claims is auto-filled by an AI operator when the host starts the game, so a room with three humans and six AI-filled seats plays the same as one with nine humans. Every phase — briefing, abatement, exchange, settlement — runs on the same server-authoritative logic as Solo, so all players see identical results: the same demand scenario, the same clearing price, the same settlement.

Only the host can start the game and advance phases; everyone else submits their own abatement plan, production level, and trading order, then waits. If the host advances before someone submits, that seat defaults to no new abatement and 100% production for the period — not an error, just the safe default.

Running a training session with the Facilitator Dashboard

For a workshop or course with several breakout groups playing at once, the Facilitator Dashboard — a small link on the Live Multiplayer entry screen — batches several ordinary rooms under one facilitator identity. Each room still works exactly as described above (each breakout group joins with its own 4-letter code, on the ordinary join screen), but the facilitator gets one screen to:

  • See every room's current phase and how many seats are claimed, at a glance.
  • Set the CBAM module on or off for every room in the cohort at once, before any of them start.
  • Start All Rooms once every group is ready, and Advance All Rooms to move every group through the compliance year together — or advance a single room on its own if one group needs more time.
Save the facilitator token

Creating a cohort gives you a facilitator code (F-XXXXX) and a facilitator token. The code alone is enough to view every room's status from any browser, but the token is required to start or advance rooms — it's shown once and isn't recoverable, so save it if you'll need to reconnect (a refreshed tab remembers it automatically; a different browser or device does not).
